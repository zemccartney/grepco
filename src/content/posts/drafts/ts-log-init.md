I've been learning typescript in earnest over the past few months, after spending the first close-to-a-decade of my career almost exclusively in "classic" javascript. I'd dabbled some on my side projects (this site among them, though the actual typescripting required here is close to nil), but with an opportunity to migrate an existing, non-trivial project at work, I wanted to have some semi-professional-seeming idea of what I was doing. To try to get things to stick more, and in the spirit of learning in public, I'm hoping to keep a log of my travels here.

More concrete (read: with code) musings to come, but to get things rolling, here's an attempt at pulling my intuitions-so-far out of my brain. I'm curious how these will age. I expect much will seem wrongheaded looking back a month or a year from now, but I find this sort of exercise, forcing yourself to explain how you think about doing something — vs. describing specific techniques — both humbling and illuminating: humbling, in that you tend to get to feeling like there's an ungodly amount left to master and what have you even learned, anyway?; illuminating, in that you tend to stumble through your own aha moments as you write, crystallizing your jumbled experiences into a mental model that's actually starting to feel useful, to start appreciating how far you've come.

Anyway:

Writing typescript effectively is an exercise in restraint. It is very tempting to chase the red squiggles; I often do when not thinking clearly about a problem or I'm overtired or said problem is particularly wracking my brain and I'm procrastinating. When I find myself whack-a-moling typescript errors, the best thing to do is STOP. Take a breath. Why are these errors occurring? What do they mean in my context? What functional requirements am I actually supporting here? What intent is most useful to capture to that end? What do we know about runtime behavior here? What do we _need_ to be true at runtime, that if we're wrong, there's nothing we can actually do? You don't want to model pathological states like you don't want to catch programmer errors; if there's a bug, the best course is to let it crash, so at least you know about it (this sums up my current intuition for using type assertions, hopefully that can make it in the log at some point). I've found the deeper I get into wrestling with the type-checker, the more detached I become from runtime reality, at which point, why am I even writing code? :) When I slow down and think a beat, I usually write less typescript (or rather, stop typing for typing's sake); squiggle-hunting tends to mean there's a root cause you haven't unearthed yet that fixing symptom after symptom can sometimes only bury deeper.

The underlying point here: I find it really hard to know if I'm doing a good job typescripting. Did I do enough? Too much? (yes, of course, it depends, fine). I'm not building against functional requirements, the only verification is quiet from the type-checker, ambiguous at best. Seems the telling is in the long run: how easy to re-understand your project coming back to it after a week or a month; how manageable refactors are; how self-explanatory the code becomes by virtue of how well you've modeled intent. The operative word: modeling. Describing. Documenting. Typescript doesn't have any impact on your running program, it's stripped away, compiled down to javascript. You're not guaranteeing a correct program, not least because you can easily lie to the type-checker. Again, always keep runtime in mind.

It's been years since I've read any of Steve McConnell's _Code Complete_, but his discussion about the power of metaphors in the opening chapters has stuck with me, how a well-fitting metaphor steers your thinking effectively and healthily (I'm sure I'm badly mis-paraphrasing his discussion; overdue for a reread).

So, to that end:

You're at the beach, the ocean tide rolling in. Building a sand castle where eventually the water will rise up and flow into or around or over your creation. The path the water takes is your choice. And opting to block it out is a perfectly fine one. But in our case, a sandcastle, high walls, but with a moat and a groove cut through a front arch so when the tide rolls in and surrounds your castle and eventually fills your moat, the water pours in through the arch and fills whatever waterways you've cut inside. As a kid, catching the tide like this: hyped!

The sand is your code. Soft, malleable, form and reform. The water is data, the flow of information, as structured by your code. Typescript is building to allow the flow of water within your construction (digging moats, trenches, grooves, whatever), that thinking about how data moves through your system becomes a chief part of its architecture. Introspective, in a word. Non-essential, but makes you think more about the interior of your construction, how it might need to grow to keep stable as the tide rises.

I'm not sold on this model. Water's an evocative metaphor for me, for some reason I find myself thinking of writing typescript as getting your types to flow through your system (I think I heard this phrase somewhere? I can't recall), like when you've done your plumbing right, inference starts to just happen vs. needing to continually write more typescript. But this metaphor doesn't seem to capture how Typescript relates to javascript, a superset that's compiled away before the time of use. Kind of? I suppose it does in the sense that cutting grooves in sand is construction by absence? I also considered construction scaffolding (apt, perhaps, given construction was McConnell's choice metaphor for creating software), but didn't love that scaffolding, I assume, is absolutely necessary to some projects, whereas typescript is never _needed_ for any project aiming to run javascript in production (extremely valuable is not the same as needed) (I could be completely wrong about this, too, I don't know anything about how buildings are made :) ). I dunno. The point here was not perfection, but to lay a reference point. Will be thinking on this.
